    

    These problems cover the topics on the midterm. Work each problem before you look at the answer, and show your steps the way you would on the exam.
    🔗

    1. Problem 1: Multiplying Two-Digit Numbers.

    Multiply without a calculator. When a part names a strategy, use that strategy.
    🔗

    (a)

    Use an area model to find \(36 \times 24\text{.}\) Label the side lengths of each piece, write the partial product inside each piece, and then add the partial products.
    🔗
    
    A large rectangle split into four smaller rectangles. The vertical dividing line is closer to the right side, and the horizontal dividing line is closer to the bottom, so the upper left piece is the largest and the lower right piece is the smallest. No labels are given.
    🔗
    Answer.
    Break \(36\) into \(30 + 6\) across the top and \(24\) into \(20 + 4\) down the side. The four pieces are
    \begin{equation*} 30 \times 20 = 600, \quad 6 \times 20 = 120, \quad 30 \times 4 = 120, \quad 6 \times 4 = 24 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Adding the partial products:
    \begin{equation*} 600 + 120 + 120 + 24 = 864 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Find \(36 \times 24\) again using a different strategy, such as breaking apart one factor or doubling and halving. Show each step.
    🔗
    Answer.
    Breaking apart \(24\) into \(20 + 4\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} 36 \times 24 = 36 \times 20 + 36 \times 4 = 720 + 144 = 864 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Doubling and halving also works, since doubling one factor and halving the other keeps the product the same:
    \begin{equation*} 36 \times 24 = 72 \times 12 = 144 \times 6 = 864 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Use compensation to find \(49 \times 32\text{:}\) change one factor to a friendlier number, multiply, and then adjust. Explain why your adjustment makes sense.
    🔗
    Answer.
    Round \(49\) up to \(50\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} 50 \times 32 = 1600 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Fifty groups of \(32\) is one more group than we want, so take one group of \(32\) back off:
    \begin{equation*} 1600 - 32 = 1568 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Check your answer to the previous part using a second strategy of your choice.
    🔗
    Answer.
    Breaking apart \(32\) into \(30 + 2\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} 49 \times 32 = 49 \times 30 + 49 \times 2 = 1470 + 98 = 1568 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    An area model with \(40 + 9\) and \(30 + 2\) gives the same result: \(1200 + 80 + 270 + 18 = 1568\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2. Problem 2: Order of Operations.

    Evaluate the following expressions step-by-step without using an electronic tool. Treat each fraction bar as a grouping symbol. Show all intermediate steps to illustrate your reasoning.
    🔗

    (a)

    \begin{equation*} \frac{3^2 + 3 \cdot 5}{2(10 - 7)^2 - 6} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Answer.
    Treat the numerator and denominator as separate groups. In the numerator, evaluate the exponent. In the denominator, simplify inside the parentheses:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{9 + 3 \cdot 5}{2(3)^2 - 6} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Multiply in the numerator and evaluate the exponent in the denominator:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{9 + 15}{2(9) - 6} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Add in the numerator and multiply in the denominator:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{24}{18 - 6} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Subtract in the denominator:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{24}{12} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Finally, divide:
    \begin{equation*} 2 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    \begin{equation*} 2 \cdot 3^2 - \frac{20 - 4^2}{2} + 7 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Answer.
    Evaluate the exponents first, including the one in the numerator of the fraction:
    \begin{equation*} 2 \cdot 9 - \frac{20 - 16}{2} + 7 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Multiply in the first term and subtract in the numerator of the fraction:
    \begin{equation*} 18 - \frac{4}{2} + 7 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Perform the division indicated by the fraction bar:
    \begin{equation*} 18 - 2 + 7 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Addition and subtraction are done from left to right, so subtract first:
    \begin{equation*} 16 + 7 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Finally, add:
    \begin{equation*} 23 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    A common mistake is to add \(2 + 7\) first, which leads to \(18 - 9 = 9\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    \begin{equation*} 60 - 2\left( \frac{4^2 - 1}{3} \right)^2 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Answer.
    Start inside the parentheses. The fraction is its own group, so begin with the exponent in its numerator:
    \begin{equation*} 60 - 2\left( \frac{16 - 1}{3} \right)^2 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Subtract in the numerator:
    \begin{equation*} 60 - 2\left( \frac{15}{3} \right)^2 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Divide to finish the work inside the parentheses:
    \begin{equation*} 60 - 2(5)^2 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Evaluate the exponent before multiplying:
    \begin{equation*} 60 - 2(25) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Multiply:
    \begin{equation*} 60 - 50 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Finally, subtract:
    \begin{equation*} 10 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The exponent applies only to the \(5\text{.}\) Multiplying \(2 \cdot 5\) first and squaring \(10\) gives \(60 - 100 = -40\text{,}\) which is incorrect.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3. Problem 3: A Number Pattern.

    The numbers below follow a pattern.
    \begin{equation*} 7, \quad 13, \quad 19, \quad 25, \quad 31, \quad 37, \quad 43, \quad \ldots \end{equation*}
    🔗

    (a)

    Describe in words how to get from one number in the list to the next.
    🔗
    Answer.
    Each number is \(6\) more than the number before it, so add \(6\) to get the next number.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    The number \(151\) is in this list. What number is four spots after \(151\text{?}\) What number is two spots before \(151\text{?}\) Explain how you found each one.
    🔗
    Answer.
    Moving four spots forward adds \(6\) four times, which is \(24\text{,}\) so the number four spots after \(151\) is \(151 + 24 = 175\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Moving two spots back subtracts \(6\) twice, which is \(12\text{,}\) so the number two spots before \(151\) is \(151 - 12 = 139\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Suppose \(x\) is a number somewhere in this list. Fill in the table, writing each entry in terms of \(x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Two spots before Previous Current Next Two spots after
    \(\rule{0pt}{0.45in}\) \(\rule{0pt}{0.45in}\) \(x\) \(\rule{0pt}{0.45in}\) \(\rule{0pt}{0.45in}\)
    Answer.
    Each step forward adds \(6\) and each step back subtracts \(6\text{,}\) so the row reads
    \begin{equation*} x - 12, \quad x - 6, \quad x, \quad x + 6, \quad x + 12 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    4. Problem 4: A Growing Spider.

    The first three cases of a tile pattern are shown below. Each case has one dark tile for the body and five legs made of light tiles.
    🔗
    
    Case 1 has one dark body tile and five light legs of one tile each, reaching up and to the left, up and to the right, down and to the left, down and to the right, and straight down, for 6 tiles. Case 2 has the same body with each leg two tiles long, for 11 tiles. Case 3 has each leg three tiles long, for 16 tiles. The diagonal legs touch at their corners.
    🔗

    (a)

    Explain how the number of tiles changes from one case to the next. Describe where the new tiles appear in the picture.
    🔗
    Answer.
    Each new case adds one tile to the end of each of the five legs, so the pattern grows by \(5\) tiles each time. The counts are \(6\text{,}\) \(11\text{,}\) and \(16\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Draw Case 4.
    🔗
    Answer.
    Case 4 has the dark body tile with five legs of four tiles each, reaching up and to the left, up and to the right, down and to the left, down and to the right, and straight down. That is \(1 + 5(4) = 21\) tiles.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    How many tiles are in Case 10? Explain how you know without drawing every case.
    🔗
    Answer.
    In Case 10, each of the five legs has \(10\) tiles, and there is one body tile, so there are \(1 + 5(10) = 51\) tiles.
    🔗
    Another way: Case 3 has \(16\) tiles, and getting to Case 10 takes \(7\) more steps of \(5\) tiles, so \(16 + 35 = 51\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    5. Problem 5: Distributing and Combining Like Terms.

    Simplify each expression by distributing and then combining like terms. When two groups are multiplied, multiply each term in the first group by each term in the second. Show each step.
    🔗

    (a)

    \begin{equation*} 4(x + 3) + 2x \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Answer.
    Distribute the \(4\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} 4x + 12 + 2x \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Group the like terms together:
    \begin{equation*} 4x + 2x + 12 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Combine the like terms:
    \begin{equation*} 6x + 12 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    \begin{equation*} 3(2x - 5) - 4x + 9 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Answer.
    Distribute the \(3\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} 6x - 15 - 4x + 9 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Group the like terms together:
    \begin{equation*} 6x - 4x - 15 + 9 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Combine the like terms:
    \begin{equation*} 2x - 6 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    \begin{equation*} 5x - (3x - 8) + 2 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Answer.
    Distribute the negative sign to both terms inside the parentheses:
    \begin{equation*} 5x - 3x + 8 + 2 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Combine the like terms:
    \begin{equation*} 2x + 10 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Subtracting \(-8\) turns it into \(+8\text{.}\) Leaving it as \(-8\) is the most common mistake on this kind of problem.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    \begin{equation*} -2(4x - 3) + 6(x + 1) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Answer.
    Distribute the \(-2\) and the \(6\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} -8x + 6 + 6x + 6 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Group the like terms together:
    \begin{equation*} -8x + 6x + 6 + 6 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Combine the like terms:
    \begin{equation*} -2x + 12 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (e)

    \begin{equation*} 3(x^2 - 2x + 4) - 2(x^2 + 5x - 1) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Answer.
    Distribute the \(3\) and the \(-2\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} 3x^2 - 6x + 12 - 2x^2 - 10x + 2 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Group the like terms together:
    \begin{equation*} 3x^2 - 2x^2 - 6x - 10x + 12 + 2 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Combine the like terms:
    \begin{equation*} x^2 - 16x + 14 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The \(-2\) multiplies every term in the second parentheses, so \(-1\) becomes \(+2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (f)

    \begin{equation*} (x + 3)(x + 5) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Answer.
    Multiply each term in the first parentheses by each term in the second:
    \begin{equation*} x \cdot x + x \cdot 5 + 3 \cdot x + 3 \cdot 5 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Simplify each product:
    \begin{equation*} x^2 + 5x + 3x + 15 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Combine the like terms:
    \begin{equation*} x^2 + 8x + 15 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (g)

    \begin{equation*} (2x - 1)(x + 4) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Answer.
    Multiply each term in the first parentheses by each term in the second:
    \begin{equation*} 2x \cdot x + 2x \cdot 4 - 1 \cdot x - 1 \cdot 4 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Simplify each product:
    \begin{equation*} 2x^2 + 8x - x - 4 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Combine the like terms:
    \begin{equation*} 2x^2 + 7x - 4 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (h)

    \begin{equation*} (x - 2)(x^2 + 3x - 1) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Answer.
    Multiply \(x\) by each of the three terms in the second parentheses, then multiply \(-2\) by each of them. That gives six products:
    \begin{equation*} x^3 + 3x^2 - x - 2x^2 - 6x + 2 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Group the like terms together:
    \begin{equation*} x^3 + 3x^2 - 2x^2 - x - 6x + 2 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Combine the like terms:
    \begin{equation*} x^3 + x^2 - 7x + 2 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    A box with two rows and three columns is a good way to keep track of all six products.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (i)

    \begin{equation*} (x + 4)(x - 1) - 3(x + 2) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Answer.
    Multiply the two binomials and distribute the \(-3\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} x^2 - x + 4x - 4 - 3x - 6 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Group the like terms together:
    \begin{equation*} x^2 - x + 4x - 3x - 4 - 6 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Combine the like terms. The \(x\) terms add up to zero:
    \begin{equation*} x^2 - 10 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    6. Problem 6: Cookie Box Prices.

    A bakery tried a different price for a box of cookies each week and recorded how many boxes it sold that week.
    🔗
    Price (dollars) Boxes sold
    \(4\) \(94\)
    \(5\) \(90\)
    \(6\) \(78\)
    \(7\) \(71\)
    \(8\) \(62\)
    \(9\) \(55\)
    \(10\) \(41\)
    \(11\) \(36\)

    (a)

    Make a scatterplot of the data on the grid. Label each axis with what it measures and its units, and choose a scale so that the points use most of the grid.
    🔗
    
    A blank grid 12 squares wide and 10 squares tall. The horizontal and vertical axes are drawn along the bottom and left edges with arrows, but there are no numbers or labels.
    🔗
    Answer.
    Put price on the horizontal axis and boxes sold on the vertical axis. One dollar per square on the horizontal axis and \(10\) boxes per square on the vertical axis fits every point. The eight points are \((4, 94)\text{,}\) \((5, 90)\text{,}\) \((6, 78)\text{,}\) \((7, 71)\text{,}\) \((8, 62)\text{,}\) \((9, 55)\text{,}\) \((10, 41)\text{,}\) and \((11, 36)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    What scale did you choose for each axis? Explain why it works for this data.
    🔗
    Answer.
    The vertical axis has to reach \(94\) with \(10\) squares, so \(10\) boxes per square works. A scale of \(5\) per square would only reach \(50\) and leave most of the points off the grid. The prices go up to \(11\) and there are \(12\) squares across, so \(1\) dollar per square works.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Describe the trend in the data. What happens to the number of boxes sold as the price goes up?
    🔗
    Answer.
    As the price goes up, the number of boxes sold goes down. The points fall close to a straight line, and sales drop by about \(8\) boxes for each extra dollar.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    7. Problem 7: Walking to the Library.

    Dani leaves home and walks toward the library, which is \(0.8\) miles away. On the way, she stops at a coffee shop \(0.3\) miles from home, arriving after \(6\) minutes. She spends \(8\) minutes at the coffee shop, then walks the rest of the way to the library at the same steady pace, which takes another \(10\) minutes. She stays at the library for \(10\) minutes to pick up a book, then a friend drives her home. The drive home takes \(4\) minutes at a steady speed.
    🔗

    (a)

    Identify the input and output quantities in this story, including units.
    🔗
    Answer.
    The input is the time since Dani left home, in minutes. The output is Dani’s distance from home, in miles.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Sketch a graph of Dani’s distance from home over time on the grid. Label each axis and choose a scale so that the whole trip fits.
    🔗
    
    A blank grid 20 squares wide and 10 squares tall. The horizontal and vertical axes are drawn along the bottom and left edges with arrows, but there are no numbers or labels.
    🔗
    Answer.
    Using \(2\) minutes per square across and \(0.1\) mile per square up, the graph connects \((0, 0)\text{,}\) \((6, 0.3)\text{,}\) \((14, 0.3)\text{,}\) \((24, 0.8)\text{,}\) \((34, 0.8)\text{,}\) and \((38, 0)\) with straight segments.
    🔗
    
    Time in minutes from 0 to 40 on the horizontal axis and distance from home in miles from 0 to 1 on the vertical axis. Straight segments connect (0, 0), (6, 0.3), (14, 0.3), (24, 0.8), (34, 0.8), and (38, 0). The two rising segments have the same steepness, and the final falling segment is much steeper.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    What do the horizontal pieces of your graph mean in the story?
    🔗
    Answer.
    From minute \(6\) to minute \(14\text{,}\) Dani is at the coffee shop, so her distance from home stays at \(0.3\) miles. From minute \(24\) to minute \(34\text{,}\) she is at the library, so her distance stays at \(0.8\) miles.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Should the two walking pieces of your graph have the same steepness? Explain using the story.
    🔗
    Answer.
    Yes. Dani walks at the same steady pace both times. She covers \(0.3\) miles in \(6\) minutes and then \(0.5\) miles in \(10\) minutes, and both are \(0.05\) miles per minute.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (e)

    Which part of your graph is steepest? What does that tell you about the story?
    🔗
    Answer.
    The drive home is steepest. Dani covers \(0.8\) miles in \(4\) minutes, which is \(0.2\) miles per minute. Walking, she covers \(0.05\) miles per minute, so the car is four times as fast as walking.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    8. Problem 8: A Camera Drone.

    A camera drone takes off from the ground and climbs at a steady rate. It hovers in place for a while to take some overhead shots. Then it descends slowly to get a better angle and hovers again. Finally, it drops quickly and lands.
    🔗

    (a)

    On the axes below, sketch a graph of the drone’s height above the ground over time. Label each axis with the quantity it shows. You do not need numbers on the axes.
    🔗
    
    A horizontal axis and a vertical axis meeting at the lower left corner, each with an arrow. There is no grid, and there are no numbers or labels.
    🔗
    Answer.
    The horizontal axis is time and the vertical axis is the drone’s height above the ground. The graph starts at the origin, rises in a straight line, stays flat, falls gently to a lower height, stays flat again, and then falls steeply back to the horizontal axis.
    🔗
    
    Time on the horizontal axis and height above the ground on the vertical axis, with no numbers. Starting at the origin, a straight segment rises, a horizontal segment shows the first hover, a gently falling segment leads to a lower horizontal segment for the second hover, and a steep segment drops back to the horizontal axis.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Should your graph start at the origin? Explain why or why not.
    🔗
    Answer.
    Yes. At the start, no time has passed and the drone is sitting on the ground, so its height is zero.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Why are some pieces of your graph horizontal? What is the drone doing during those times?
    🔗
    Answer.
    The drone is hovering in place, so its height is not changing as time goes on. The second flat piece is lower than the first because the drone came down partway before hovering again.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    How does your graph show the difference between the slow descent and the quick landing?
    🔗
    Answer.
    Both pieces go down, but the landing is much steeper. A steeper piece means the height changes more each second, so the drone is dropping faster.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    9. Problem 9: A Rain Barrel.

    A family collects rainwater in a barrel and uses it to water their garden. The graph shows the amount of water in the barrel, in gallons, over two weeks. The input is time, in days.
    🔗
    
    Time in days from 0 to 14 on the horizontal axis and water in gallons from 0 to 60 on the vertical axis. The graph is made of straight segments connecting (0, 10), (2, 10), (4, 40), (6, 40), (8, 20), (9, 20), (10, 50), and (14, 30).
    🔗

    (a)

    Identify the independent and dependent variables, including units.
    🔗
    Answer.
    The independent variable is time, in days. The dependent variable is the amount of water in the barrel, in gallons.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    How much water was in the barrel at the start? Where does this appear on the graph?
    🔗
    Answer.
    There were \(10\) gallons at the start. This is the vertical intercept, the point \((0, 10)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    During which days was the amount of water not changing? What might have been happening during those days?
    🔗
    Answer.
    The amount stayed the same from day \(0\) to day \(2\text{,}\) from day \(4\) to day \(6\text{,}\) and from day \(8\) to day \(9\text{.}\) During those days it did not rain and the family did not use any water from the barrel.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    The barrel filled during two different stretches of time. During which stretch did it fill faster? Compare the rates to justify your answer.
    🔗
    Answer.
    From day \(2\) to day \(4\text{,}\) the barrel gained \(30\) gallons in \(2\) days, which is \(15\) gallons per day. From day \(9\) to day \(10\text{,}\) it gained \(30\) gallons in \(1\) day, which is \(30\) gallons per day. The barrel filled twice as fast from day \(9\) to day \(10\text{,}\) which is why that piece of the graph is steeper.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (e)

    (a)

    Complete the table.

    Minutes, \(m\) \(0\) \(10\) \(20\) \(30\) \(40\) \(50\)
    Cost, \(C\) (dollars) \(0\) \(4\) \(\rule{0pt}{0.35in}\) \(\rule{0pt}{0.35in}\) \(\rule{0pt}{0.35in}\) \(\rule{0pt}{0.35in}\)
    Answer.
